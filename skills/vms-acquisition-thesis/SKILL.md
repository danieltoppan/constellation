# VMS Acquisition Thesis

Produces a one-page, evidence-backed thesis on a single vertical market software (VMS) company. Used after `vms-target-screener`, or on any company the user names. The thesis must be something the user can defend out loud when an M&A professional pushes on it.

## Inputs

- Company name and website (required). If a screener row exists, use it as a starting point, then re-verify.
- Optional: the buyer context (for example a specific Constellation operating group), the user's angle or hunch.

## Rules

1. Every factual claim gets a link to a page you actually opened. Search snippets are not sources.
2. Separate facts, estimates and opinions. Estimates say their method. Opinions sit under "Why it fits" or "Risks".
3. If sources disagree (founding year, customer count, country count), show both and note the conflict. Do not pick one silently.
4. Check for name collisions: confirm domain, HQ and product before using any database page.
5. Never invent revenue, margins, churn or customer counts. Missing = listed under "What I could not find".
6. Plain language, short sentences, no hype words ("leading", "innovative", "best-in-class") unless quoting the company's own claim and labelling it as a claim.
7. Length: 400 to 650 words plus the snapshot table and sources.
8. No em dashes. Use commas, colons or parentheses.

## Research checklist (do all, in this order)

1. Company site: home, product, about/history, leadership, customers, pricing, news
2. Ownership: search "<company> acquired", "<company> investment", "<company> portfolio"; check press releases
3. Leadership: who founded it, who runs it now, tenure, recent changes (press, LinkedIn pages if reachable)
4. Market: two or three rivals, who owns them, any share figures (with year)
5. Scale: headcount, customer counts, locations, countries (each with source and date)
6. Triggers in last 24 months: retirements, promotions, new ventures, anniversaries, product launches, rival deals
7. Buyer fit: does the buyer already own a rival or adjacent business (possible bolt-on), or would this be a new platform

## Output format

### <Company> (<product name>)

One sentence: what the software does, for whom, in plain words.

**Snapshot**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Industry served | |
| HQ and offices | |
| Founded | year, founder |
| Leadership | current leader, tenure, recent changes |
| Size | headcount (source, date) |
| Customers | counts or scale claims (source, date) |
| Revenue | "Not found" or estimate with method |
| Ownership | what was found, and how it was checked |
| Main rivals | names and their owners |

**Why it fits** (3 to 5 bullets, each tied to a criterion: vertical focus, mission-critical, recurring revenue, market position, customer breadth, longevity)

**Why now** (1 to 3 bullets: the sourced trigger events)

**Deal shape** (one line: platform, bolt-on to an existing group, or carve-out of a product line)

**Risks** (3 bullets, honest: market decline, concentration, competition, technology debt, seller pricing expectations)

**What I could not find** (bullets; this is required and should rarely be empty)

**Likely pushback and answers** (3 pairs: the toughest question an M&A lead would ask, then a short, honest answer that admits uncertainty where it exists)

**First-call questions** (3: ownership, owner's 5 to 10 year plans, what matters to them in a partner)

**How it was found** (2 to 3 sentences describing the actual sourcing path: list source, filters, what made it stand out. Do not dress it up.)

**Sources** (bulleted links, one per page used)

## Final check

- Every number has a source and date, or is labelled as an estimate
- Conflicts shown, not hidden
- "What I could not find" is filled in
- No claim that would embarrass the user if the owner read it
